  Exercise 14 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A stone tied to the end of a string 80 cm long is whirled in a horizontal circle with a constant speed. If the stone makes 14 revolutions in 25 s, what is the magnitude and direction of acceleration of the stone ?
  A: Radius $r = 80$ cm = $0.8$ m. Frequency $\nu = \frac{14}{25}$ Hz. Angular speed $\omega = 2\pi\nu = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times \frac{14}{25} = 3.52$ rad/s. Centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r = (3.52)^2 \times 0.8 \approx 9.9$ m/s$^2$. The direction is along the string towards the centre.

  Exercise 15 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: An aircraft executes a horizontal loop of radius 1.00 km with a steady speed of 900 km/h. Compare its centripetal acceleration with the acceleration due to gravity.
  A: $r = 1000$ m, $v = 900$ km/h = $900 \times \frac{5}{18} = 250$ m/s. Centripetal acceleration $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{250^2}{1000} = 62.5$ m/s$^2$. Ratio $\frac{a_c}{g} = \frac{62.5}{9.8} \approx 6.38$.

  Exercise 16 (conceptual, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: Read each statement below carefully and state, with reasons, if it is true or false : (a) The net acceleration of a particle in circular motion is always along the radius of the circle towards the centre (b) The velocity vector of a particle at a point is always along the tangent to the path of the particle at that point (c) The acceleration vector of a particle in uniform circular motion averaged over one cycle is a null vector
  A: (a) False. This is only true for uniform circular motion. In non-uniform circular motion, there is also a tangential component of acceleration. (b) True. Velocity is always tangential to the path. (c) True. In uniform circular motion, the acceleration vector has constant magnitude but its direction changes continuously. Over one full cycle, the sum of these vectors is zero.

  Q: The position of a particle is given by $\mathbf{r} = 3.0t \hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2.0t^2 \hat{\mathbf{j}} + 4.0 \hat{\mathbf{k}}$ m where $t$ is in seconds and the coefficients have the proper units for $\mathbf{r}$ to be in metres. (a) Find the $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ of the particle? (b) What is the magnitude and direction of velocity of the particle at $t = 2.0$ s ?
  A: (a) $\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = 3.0 \hat{\mathbf{i}} - 4.0t \hat{\mathbf{j}}$. $\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = -4.0 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$. (b) At $t = 2.0$ s, $\mathbf{v} = 3.0 \hat{\mathbf{i}} - 8.0 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$. Magnitude $v = \sqrt{3^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{73} \approx 8.54$ m/s. Direction $\theta = \tan^{-1}(\frac{-8}{3}) \approx -69.4^\circ$ with the $x$-axis.

  Exercise 18 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A particle starts from the origin at $t = 0$ s with a velocity of $10.0 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$ m/s and moves in the $x$-$y$ plane with a constant acceleration of $(8.0\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2.0\hat{\mathbf{j}})$ m s$^{-2}$. (a) At what time is the $x$-coordinate of the particle 16 m? What is the $y$-coordinate of the particle at that time? (b) What is the speed of the particle at the time ?
  A: (a) $x(t) = x_0 + v_{0x}t + \frac{1}{2}a_x t^2 \Rightarrow 16 = 0 + 0 + \frac{1}{2}(8)t^2 \Rightarrow 4t^2 = 16 \Rightarrow t = 2$ s. $y(t) = y_0 + v_{0y}t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2 = 0 + 10(2) + \frac{1}{2}(2)(2^2) = 20 + 4 = 24$ m. (b) $\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t = 10\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (8\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2\hat{\mathbf{j}})(2) = 16\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 14\hat{\mathbf{j}}$. Speed $v = \sqrt{16^2 + 14^2} = \sqrt{256 + 196} = \sqrt{452} \approx 21.26$ m/s.

  A: Magnitude of $\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \hat{\mathbf{j}}$ is $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, direction is $45^\circ$ with $x$-axis. Magnitude of $\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \hat{\mathbf{j}}$ is $\sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}$, direction is $-45^\circ$ with $x$-axis. Component of $\mathbf{A}$ along $\mathbf{B}$ is $\frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{|\mathbf{B}|}$. Along $\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $\frac{(2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}}) \cdot (\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \hat{\mathbf{j}})}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2+3}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}}$. Along $\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \hat{\mathbf{j}}$: $\frac{(2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}}) \cdot (\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \hat{\mathbf{j}})}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2-3}{\sqrt{2}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$.

  Exercise 17 (derivation, easy)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A nucleus is at rest in the laboratory frame of reference. Show that if it disintegrates into two smaller nuclei the products must move in opposite directions.
  A: According to the law of conservation of linear momentum, the total momentum of an isolated system remains constant. Initially, the nucleus is at rest, so its initial momentum $\vec{p}_i = 0$. After disintegration, let the masses of the two products be $m_1$ and $m_2$ with velocities $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ respectively. The final momentum is $\vec{p}_f = m_1\vec{v}_1 + m_2\vec{v}_2$. Since $\vec{p}_i = \vec{p}_f$, we have $0 = m_1\vec{v}_1 + m_2\vec{v}_2$, which implies $m_1\vec{v}_1 = -m_2\vec{v}_2$. This shows that the momentum vectors are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, meaning the two nuclei must move in opposite directions along the same line.

  A: Given $m = 0.25$ kg, $r = 1.5$ m, and frequency $\nu = 40$ rev/min $= \frac{40}{60} = \frac{2}{3}$ rev/s. Angular velocity $\omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi(\frac{2}{3}) = \frac{4\pi}{3}$ rad/s. Grade 11 - Chapter 14: Waves
------------------------------------------------------------
  Exercise 1 (numerical, easy)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A string of mass 2.50 kg is under a tension of 200 N. The length of the stretched string is 20.0 m. If the transverse jerk is struck at one end of the string, how long does the disturbance take to reach the other end?
  A: Mass $M = 2.50$ kg, Tension $T = 200$ N, Length $L = 20.0$ m. Linear mass density $\mu = M/L = 2.50/20.0 = 0.125$ kg/m. Speed of transverse wave $v = \sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{200/0.125} = \sqrt{1600} = 40$ m/s. Time taken $t = L/v = 20.0/40 = 0.5$ s.

  Exercise 2 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A stone dropped from the top of a tower of height 300 m splashes into the water of a pond near the base of the tower. When is the splash heard at the top given that the speed of sound in air is 340 m s$^{-1}$? ($g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$)
  A: Height $h = 300$ m. Time for stone to fall $t_1 = \sqrt{2h/g} = \sqrt{2 \times 300 / 9.8} \approx 7.82$ s. Time for sound to travel up $t_2 = h/v_s = 300/340 \approx 0.88$ s. Total time $t = t_1 + t_2 = 7.82 + 0.88 = 8.7$ s.

  Exercise 3 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A steel wire has a length of 12.0 m and a mass of 2.10 kg. What should be the tension in the wire so that speed of a transverse wave on the wire equals the speed of sound in dry air at 20 °C = 343 m s$^{-1}$.
  A: $L = 12.0$ m, $M = 2.10$ kg, $v = 343$ m/s. $\mu = M/L = 2.10/12.0 = 0.175$ kg/m. Since $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$, $T = v^2 \mu = (343)^2 \times 0.175 \approx 2.06 \times 10^4$ N.

  Exercise 4 (conceptual, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: Use the formula $v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$ to explain why the speed of sound in air (a) is independent of pressure, (b) increases with temperature, (c) increases with humidity.
  A: (a) From ideal gas law $P/\rho = RT/M$. Thus $v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$. At constant temperature, $v$ is independent of $P$. (b) $v \propto \sqrt{T}$, so speed increases with temperature. (c) Humidity decreases the density $\rho$ of air. Since $v \propto 1/\sqrt{\rho}$, speed increases.

  Exercise 5 (conceptual, hard)
  ------------------------------
  Q: Examine if the following functions for $y$ can possibly represent a travelling wave: (a) $(x - vt)^2$, (b) $\log [(x + vt)/x_0]$, (c) $1/(x + vt)$.
  A: A function represents a physical wave only if it is finite and continuous for all $x$ and $t$. (a) $(x-vt)^2$ is not bounded as $x \to \infty$. (b) $\log$ is not bounded. (c) $1/(x+vt)$ is singular at $x = -vt$. Thus, none represent a physical travelling wave.

  Q: A long solenoid with $15$ turns per cm has a small loop of area $2.0 \text{ cm}^2$ placed inside the solenoid normal to its axis. If the current carried by the solenoid changes steadily from $2.0 \text{ A}$ to $4.0 \text{ A}$ in $0.1 \text{ s}$, what is the induced emf in the loop while the current is changing?
  A: Given $n = 1500 \text{ turns/m}$, $A = 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2$, and $\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{4.0 - 2.0}{0.1} = 20 \text{ A/s}$. (b) $e = Blv = 0.3 \times 0.02 \times 0.01 = 0.6 \times 10^{-4} \text{ V}$. The voltage lasts for $t = \frac{l}{v} = \frac{0.08}{0.01} = 8 \text{ s}$.

  Q: A $1.0 \text{ m}$ long metallic rod is rotated with an angular frequency of $400 \text{ rad s}^{-1}$ about an axis normal to the rod passing through its one end. Calculate the emf developed between the centre and the ring.

  Q: Figure 8.5 shows a capacitor made of two circular plates each of radius 12 cm, and separated by 5.0 cm. The capacitor is being charged by an external source. The charging current is constant and equal to 0.15A. (a) Calculate the capacitance and the rate of change of potential difference between the plates.   Q: Find the (a) maximum frequency, and (b) minimum wavelength of X-rays produced by $30 \text{ kV}$ electrons.

  Q: Suppose you are given a chance to repeat the alpha-particle scattering experiment using a thin sheet of solid hydrogen in place of the gold foil. (Hydrogen is a solid at temperatures below 14 K.) What results do you expect?
  A: In the alpha-particle scattering experiment, the scattering is caused by the Coulombic repulsion between the positive charge of the nucleus and the alpha particle. For significant backscattering, the mass of the target nucleus must be much larger than the mass of the alpha particle. Since a hydrogen nucleus (proton) has a mass of approximately $1$ u, which is much smaller than the mass of an alpha particle (approximately $4$ u), the alpha particle will not be scattered back. Instead, the hydrogen nuclei would be knocked forward. Thus, no large-angle scattering would be observed.

  Exercise 3 (numerical, easy)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A difference of 2.3 eV separates two energy levels in an atom. What is the frequency of radiation emitted when the atom make a transition from the upper level to the lower level?
  A: Given the energy difference $\Delta E = 2.3$ eV. We use the relation $\Delta E = h\nu$. Converting energy to Joules: $\Delta E = 2.3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J. The frequency is $\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{2.3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 5.55 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.

  Exercise 4 (numerical, easy)
  ------------------------------
  Q: The ground state energy of hydrogen atom is -13.6 eV. What are the kinetic and potential energies of the electron in this state?
  A: For an electron in a Bohr orbit, the total energy $E$, kinetic energy $K$, and potential energy $U$ are related by: $K = -E$ and $U = 2E$. Given $E = -13.6$ eV, we have: $K = -(-13.6) = 13.6$ eV and $U = 2(-13.6) = -27.2$ eV.

  Exercise 5 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: A hydrogen atom initially in the ground level absorbs a photon, which excites it to the $n = 4$ level. Determine the wavelength and frequency of photon.
  A: Energy of ground state ($n=1$), $E_1 = -13.6$ eV. Energy of $n=4$ level, $E_4 = \frac{-13.6}{4^2} = -0.85$ eV. The energy of the absorbed photon is $\Delta E = E_4 - E_1 = -0.85 - (-13.6) = 12.75$ eV. Frequency $\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{12.75 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 3.08 \times 10^{15}$ Hz. Wavelength $\lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{3.08 \times 10^{15}} \approx 9.7 \times 10^{-8}$ m or 97 nm.

  Exercise 6 (numerical, medium)
  ------------------------------
  Q: (a) Using the Bohr’s model calculate the speed of the electron in a hydrogen atom in the $n = 1, 2,$ and $3$ levels. (b) Calculate the orbital period in each of these levels.
  A: (a) Speed $v_n = \frac{e^2}{2\epsilon_0 nh} = \frac{v_1}{n}$. For $n=1$, $v_1 \approx 2.18 \times 10^6$ m/s. For $n=2$, $v_2 = \frac{v_1}{2} \approx 1.09 \times 10^6$ m/s. For $n=1$, $T_1 = \frac{2\pi \times 5.3 \times 10^{-11}}{2.18 \times 10^6} \approx 1.53 \times 10^{-16}$ s. For $n=2$, $T_2 = 2^3 T_1 \approx 1.22 \times 10^{-15}$ s. For $n=3$, $T_3 = 3^3 T_1 \approx 4.13 \times 10^{-15}$ s.

  Q: A 12.5 eV electron beam is used to bombard gaseous hydrogen at room temperature. What series of wavelengths will be emitted?

Calculating excitation energies: $E_2 - E_1 = 10.2$ eV, $E_3 - E_1 = 12.09$ eV, and $E_4 - E_1 = 12.75$ eV. Thus, the atom can be excited up to the $n=3$ level.   Exercise 9 (numerical, medium)

  Q: In accordance with the Bohr’s model, find the quantum number that characterises the earth’s revolution around the sun in an orbit of radius $1.5 \times 10^{11}$ m with orbital speed $3 \times 10^4$ m/s. (Mass of earth $= 6.0 \times 10^{24}$ kg.)
